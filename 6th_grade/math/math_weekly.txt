# 6th Grade Math Guide: Understanding the Equation y = 100 - 3x

## Introduction

Welcome to our math lesson! Today, we're going to learn about equations and how they can help us solve everyday problems. Specifically, we’ll explore the equation \( y = 100 - 3x \). This equation helps us understand how a quantity changes over time. 

Let's break it down:
- **\( y \)** represents the amount we have left.
- **\( x \)** represents the number of days.
- **100** is the starting amount.
- **3** is the amount we lose or use each day.

We’ll use a fun example involving LEGO bricks to see how this equation works!

## Practical Example: LEGO Bricks

Imagine you have a total of 100 LEGO bricks. Each day, you use 3 LEGO bricks to build different things. We can use the equation \( y = 100 - 3x \) to find out how many LEGO bricks you have left at any given day.

### Step-by-Step Example:

1. **Day 0 (when you haven’t used any LEGO bricks yet):**
   - Plug in \( x = 0 \) into the equation: 
     \[
     y = 100 - 3 \times 0
     \]
   - Simplify:
     \[
     y = 100
     \]
   - So, you have 100 LEGO bricks left.

2. **Day 1 (after using LEGO bricks for 1 day):**
   - Plug in \( x = 1 \) into the equation:
     \[
     y = 100 - 3 \times 1
     \]
   - Simplify:
     \[
     y = 100 - 3 = 97
     \]
   - You have 97 LEGO bricks left.

3. **Day 2 (after using LEGO bricks for 2 days):**
   - Plug in \( x = 2 \) into the equation:
     \[
     y = 100 - 3 \times 2
     \]
   - Simplify:
     \[
     y = 100 - 6 = 94
     \]
   - You have 94 LEGO bricks left.

4. **Day 3 (after using LEGO bricks for 3 days):**
   - Plug in \( x = 3 \) into the equation:
     \[
     y = 100 - 3 \times 3
     \]
   - Simplify:
     \[
     y = 100 - 9 = 91
     \]
   - You have 91 LEGO bricks left.

### Practice Problems:

1. **Day 5:**
   - How many LEGO bricks do you have left?
   - \( y = 100 - 3 \times 5 \)
   - Simplify and solve.

2. **Day 7:**
   - How many LEGO bricks do you have left?
   - \( y = 100 - 3 \times 7 \)
   - Simplify and solve.

## Graphing the Equation

Now let's see what the equation looks like when we graph it. 

### Creating the Graph:

1. **Draw the Axes:**
   - Draw a horizontal line (the x-axis) and a vertical line (the y-axis). 

2. **Label the Axes:**
   - The x-axis is the number of days.
   - The y-axis is the number of LEGO bricks left.

3. **Plot Points:**
   - Day 0: \( (0, 100) \)
   - Day 1: \( (1, 97) \)
   - Day 2: \( (2, 94) \)
   - Day 3: \( (3, 91) \)

4. **Connect the Dots:**
   - Draw a straight line through the points. This line shows how the number of LEGO bricks decreases over time.

### Graph Example:
```
   | 
100|* (0, 100)
   |
 90|      *
   |        *
 80|          *
   |
   +----------------
     0  1  2  3  4  (days)
```

The line slopes downward because each day, you’re using up LEGO bricks, so the amount left decreases.

## Conclusion

By understanding the equation \( y = 100 - 3x \) and graphing it, we can see how the number of LEGO bricks decreases over time. Equations like this are powerful tools that help us solve real-world problems and make predictions about different situations.

Try using this equation with other examples, like how much money you have left if you spend a certain amount each week, or how much time is left if you spend a certain amount of time each day on a project. Keep practicing and have fun with math!

Activity 1: Analize your bedroom doorframe.

Objective: Students will identify and measure angles formed by the vertical and horizontal parts of a doorframe.

Materials Needed:

Protractor
Ruler
Notebook and pencil

What type of angles did you find (acute, right, obtuse)?

This activity will help students delve into the practical application of angles in everyday structures!


Activity 2: Identifying Internal Angles of Geometric Shapes

Objective: Students will learn to calculate and identify the internal angles of various geometric shapes.

Materials Needed:

Protractor
Ruler
Graph paper
Notebook and pencil
Instructions:

1. Square:

* Draw a square on graph paper.
* Measure each internal angle using a protractor.
* Calculate the total internal angles: 4×90°=360°. Discuss why each angle is 90°

2. Rectangle:

* Draw a rectangle on graph paper.
* Measure each internal angle.
* Confirm the total internal angles: 4×90°=360°. Discuss the similarities with the square.

3. Triangle:

* Draw a triangle on graph paper.
* Measure each internal angle and calculate the total: 𝐴 + 𝐵 + 𝐶 = 180°. Discuss how the angles relate to each other.

4. Relationship between a Circle and a Square:

* Draw a square that fits inside a circle (circumscribed circle).
* Discuss how the circle has no angles and its circumference relates to the square's dimensions. Consider how the square's angles fit within the circular shape.

Reflection Questions:

* What patterns did you notice in the angles of squares and rectangles?
* How do the angles of the triangle differ from those of the other shapes?
* Why is understanding internal angles important in geometry?

This activity will help students delve into the relationships between different shapes and enhance their understanding of internal angles!

Week 10 - Number Line and properties of Integers
    [3. The Number System (NS)]


The number line
Is a visual way to represent numbers. It is a straight line with numbers placed at equal intervals. The center of the number line is zero (0), and numbers to the right of zero are positive, while numbers to the left are negative.

Here's a simple number line for reference:
<--- -3 --- -2 --- -1 --- 0 --- 1 --- 2 --- 3 --->

- Positive numbers: These are numbers greater than zero (e.g., 1, 2, 3, etc.).
- Negative numbers: These are numbers less than zero (e.g., -1, -2, -3, etc.).
- Zero (0): Zero is neither positive nor negative. It separates positive and negative numbers.

Activity 1, Integer Number Line 
    Draw a number line ranging from -10 to +10.
    Place integers they encounter in their daily life (e.g., temperatures, scores) on the number line.
    Discuss how integers can represent both positive and negative values.


- Properties of Integers:

    Commutative Property:
        Definition: Changing the order of numbers does not change the sum or product.
        Examples: 3 + 5 = 5 + 3 and 4 × 2 = 2 × 4
        In general we can say that: 
            a + b = b + a
            a * b = b * a

        Practical Example: If you have 3 apples and 5 oranges, you can count 5 oranges first or 3 apples first, and you will have 8 fruits either way.

    Associative Property:
        Definition: Changing the grouping of numbers does not change the sum or product.
        Example:  ( 2 + 3 ) + 4 = 2 + ( 3 + 4) and  ( 2 × 3 ) × 4 = 2 × ( 3 × 4)
        In general we can say that: 
            a + b + c = (a + b) + c = a + (b + c)
            a * b * c = (a * b) * c = a * (b * c)
        Practical Example: When baking, if you mix 2 cups of flour and 3 cups of sugar, then add 4 eggs, it doesn’t matter if you mix the flour and sugar first or add the eggs later.

        Beware: while mixing multiplication and addition
            (3 + 4) * 5 != 3 + (4 * 5)
                 7  * 5 != 3 + 20
                     35 != 23

    Identity Property:
        Definition: The sum of any integer and zero is the integer; the product of any integer and one is the integer.
        Example: 5 + 0 = 5 and 7 × 1 = 7
        Practical Example: If you have 7 dollars and spend 0 dollars, you still have 7 dollars.

    Distributive Property:
        Definition: Multiplying a number by a sum is the same as doing each multiplication separately.
        Example:  3 * (2 + 4) = (3 * 2) + (3 * 4)
        Practical Example: If you buy 3 packs of gum at $2 each and 3 packs at $4 each, you can add the prices before multiplying, or multiply separately.

Week 12 - Formulas and Area
    [4. Expressions and Equations (EE)]

    A formula is a mathematical expression that describes a relationship between different variables.
    It provides a way to calculate a value or to show how different quantities are related.

    Components of a Formula
        Variables: Letters that represent unknown values (e.g., 𝑥, 𝑦, 𝑎).
        Constants: Fixed values (e.g., 2, 3.14).
        Operators: Symbols that show operations (e.g., + for addition, - for subtraction, * for multiplication, and / for division).

    Examples of Formulas
        Area of square: a^2
        Meaning: Multiply the length of one side (a) by itself.

        Area of Rectanle: a * b
        Meaning: Multiply the length (a) by the width (b)

        Area of Triangle: 1/2 * 𝑏 × ℎ
        Meaning: Multiply the base (𝑏) by the height (ℎ), then divide by 2.

        Area of Circle:
        𝐴 = 𝜋 (𝑟^2)
        Meaning: Multiply 𝜋 (approximately 3.14) by the square of the radius (𝑟).


    Visualizing Area
        Grids: One way to visualize area is by using a grid. Imagine a rectangle divided into small squares. 
        Each square represents one square unit. The total number of squares inside the rectangle equals its area.

    Units of Area
        Area is always measured in square units because we are covering a surface. For example:

        A square that is 1 meter on each side has an area of 1m^2  (1 square meter).
        If you have a rectangle that is 3 meters long and 2 meters wide, its area would be 
        3×2=6m^2(6 square meters).
    
    Conclusion
        In summary, area is a fundamental concept in geometry that measures the space inside shapes. 
        It's essential for practical applications in everyday life. 
        By learning the formulas for calculating area, you can easily find out how much surface space a shape occupies.

Week 21 - Credit Cards
    [8. Financial Education (FE)]

    https://youtu.be/8emCSAYc-Cg?feature=shared

    A credit card is a small plastic card that lets you borrow money from a bank to buy things. 
    When you use a credit card, you are agreeing to pay back the money you borrowed, usually with some extra money called interest 
    if you don't pay it back quickly.

    Here's how to use a credit card:
        1. **Get a Credit Card**: 
            You need to apply for a credit card from a bank. If they approve your application, they will send you a card.

        2. **Use the Card to Buy Things**: 
            You can use your credit card to buy things in stores or online. Just give the card to the cashier or enter the card details online.

        3. **Receive a Bill**: 
            Each month, you will get a bill that shows how much money you borrowed with your credit card.

        4. **Pay the Bill**: 
            You need to pay back the money you borrowed. 
            If you pay the full amount by the due date, you won't have to pay extra money (interest). 
            If you only pay part of the bill, you will have to pay interest on the remaining amount.

        5. **Keep Track of Spending**: 
            It's important to keep track of how much you spend with your credit card so you don't borrow more money than
            you can pay back.

    Using a credit card responsibly can help you build a good credit history, which is important for borrowing money in the future, 
    like for buying a car or a house.

    Physical Characteristics
        It's a small plastic card. 
        It has four key elements:
            - A name
            - A unique card number (16 digits)
            - An expiration date (usually in the MM/YY format)
            - A security code (A 3 digit number). 
        Most common credit cards would be Visa or MasterCard, so if you see their logo in a card you know it's a credit card

    Differences with a Debit card:
        A debit card, on the other hand, is also a small plastic card, but it takes money directly from your bank account when 
        you buy things. You don't borrow money with a debit card, so you don't have to pay interest. 
        The main difference is that a credit card lets you borrow money, while a debit card uses the money you already have 
        in your bank account.

Week 22 - Investment 101
    [8. Financial Education (FE)]


https://youtu.be/8JSGKQiwafc?si=gOOR4S0hnhiS-gIF

What is Investment?
Investment is when you put your money into something with the hope that it will grow over time. 
Imagine you have $10, and you decide to lend it to a friend. Your friend promises to give it back 
to you later, but also gives you an extra $2 as a thank-you. 
That $2 is your return on investment. In the world of finance, people invest their money in many 
different ways to try to make more money.

Why Do People Invest?
- Make Money Grow: You invest to help your money grow faster than if you just kept it in a piggy bank.
- Prepare for the Future: People invest to save up for big goals, like college, a house,  or even 
  retirement (when they stop working).
- Beat Inflation: Over time, things get more expensive, and the value of money goes down. 
  Investment can help you keep up with rising prices.


Different Ways to Invest

There are many ways people can invest their money. Here are a few basic ones:

1. Stocks
    What Are They?: A stock is like owning a small piece of a company. When you buy stock, you're becoming a shareholder in that company.
    How Do You Make Money?: If the company does well, the stock price goes up, and you can sell it for more than you bought it for. Some companies also give out money to their shareholders, called dividends.
    Example: Imagine you buy a stock in a company that makes your favorite video game. If the company does well, the stock might go up in value.

2. Bonds
    What Are They?: A bond is like a loan. You lend your money to a company or government, and they promise to pay you back later with some extra money (called interest).
    How Do You Make Money?: The government or company pays you interest while they hold onto your money. When the bond "matures," they give you your original money back.
    Example: If you buy a bond for $100, they might pay you $5 a year until the bond matures. After a few years, they give you the $100 back.

3. Mutual Funds
    What Are They?: A mutual fund is a collection of stocks, bonds, and other investments grouped together. Instead of buying just one stock, you buy a share of many different investments all at once.
    How Do You Make Money?: The fund is managed by experts who try to grow your money by picking good investments. As the investments grow, so does the value of your share in the fund.
    Example: Think of a mutual fund like a basket filled with a bunch of different fruits (stocks and bonds). Instead of picking just one fruit (stock), you're getting a mix of different kinds, which helps reduce risk.

4. Real Estate
    What Is It?: Real estate is about investing in property, like houses or buildings. People buy property with the hope that it will increase in value over time.
    How Do You Make Money?: If the property goes up in value, you can sell it for a higher price. Sometimes, people also make money by renting out their property to others.
    Example: Imagine you buy a house for $200,000, and a few years later, its value increases to $250,000. You could sell it for a profit.

Risk and Reward
    One important thing to know about investing is that it comes with risk. This means you might lose some 
    or all of your money. Generally, the riskier the investment, the higher the potential reward (or the 
    chance of making more money).

    Low Risk: Bonds and savings accounts usually have lower risks but also lower rewards.
    High Risk: Stocks and real estate can have higher rewards, but they can also lose money if the company 
    or property doesn't do well.

How to Start Investing
    You might be thinking, "But I’m only in 6th grade! How can I invest?" 
    That’s a great question! Most people start investing when they’re older, but there are ways to begin 
    learning now. Here are a few steps:

    Learn: The first step is to keep learning about how money works, how different investments work, and
    what makes them risky or safe.
    Save: It’s important to save money first. You can start by saving your allowance, birthday money, 
    or money you earn from doing chores.
    Start Small: When you’re older, you can invest a small amount of money through platforms that let 
    you buy stocks or bonds (with a parent or guardian’s help).
    Talk to Adults: If you're interested in investing, talk to your parents or a trusted adult about 
    what they know. They might be able to help you set up an account when you’re ready.

Quiz Time!
    Let’s test your knowledge! Here are some questions to see how well you understand investing:

    What is a stock?
    How do bonds work?
    Why do people invest in real estate?
    What is a mutual fund?

Conclusion: Why is Investing Important?
    Investing is one of the best ways to help your money grow, especially if you start early. 
    By learning how to invest wisely, you can build wealth over time and have more financial freedom 
    in the future.

    Remember: Investing isn't a "get-rich-quick" way to make money. It’s about being patient, 
    making smart decisions, and thinking long-term!
